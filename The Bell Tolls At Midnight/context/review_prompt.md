### Witcher preparation doctrine (CRITICAL for quest / combat planning)
Geralt does not walk into a fight blind. Monsters differ; the wrong oil, wrong hour, or wrong assumption kills you. Understanding the creature is half the battle. Party planning scenes must feel like that:

1. Investigation is not optional flavor—it is survival. Before steel, the party must try to name what they face: how it dies, what it wants, what binds it, what hour it walks, what fails against it.
2. Prep is specific, not generic gear lists. Show arguments over oils by class, wards, hours, anchors, bait, retreat conditions—tied to hypotheses about THIS monster/curse, not a shopping checklist.
3. Uncertainty creates tension. If the spirit could be Wraith / Cursed / Umbra-grade (or equivalent), guessing wrong wastes the only window. Let fear of misclassification drive the plan.
4. Different mouths, different theories. A fighter wants kill-prep now; a scholar needs one clean read; a healer fears scarring the ground; a scout brings rumor and tracks. The debate IS the investigation.
5. Consequences of ignorance must be felt. Past teams failed because they didn’t understand; survivors scream partial truths; wrong methods leave haunt-scar. Prep scenes earn combat scenes.
6. Do NOT let institutions or convenience erase this. A priest/prelate may push “just destroy it,” but the party’s craft truth remains: without understanding, destruction may fail or make it worse. That clash is productive—don’t collapse it into one correct speech.
7. Show prep through work and argument (notes on the floor, salt, maps with gaps, conflicting reports)—not through a character lecturing the reader on monster taxonomy.

When revising planning scenes, ask: “Would Geralt leave for this contract yet—or would he still be asking what it eats, when it hunts, and which oil it hates?”

### Non-negotiables
1. Conversation carries plot, worldbuilding, and character. No “as you know” dumps. Characters who share history talk in fragments, assumptions, and refusals—not lectures.
2. Distinct voices. No interchangeable aphorisms (“X gives Y a shape,” “panic burns daylight”). Peasant blunt ≠ priest mild ≠ prelate bureaucratic ≠ mage nervous ≠ fighter clipped.
3. Mystery over spoilers. Effect before cause. Conflicting accounts can coexist. Do not let a character neatly name the theme or the conspiracy unless that character would actually say it under pressure.
4. Show, don’t tell. Clothing/objects imply role. Environment tells story. Max ~two concrete images per paragraph.
5. Moral fog. Both sides can be right/wrong. Choices hurt someone. Payoff is moral/emotional, not tidy victory speech.
6. Canon / scripture / seals: sound general and deniable as liturgy. Never let holy text read like a personal threat or AI fortune cookie.
7. Backstory stays in character files unless a line earns a fragment. Shared history = touchstones, not confession scenes.
8. Cut AI tells: symmetrical quips, theme taglines, shopping-list dialogue, over-explained emotion, narrator moralizing, redundant object lists.

### Elden Ring / fractured lore rules
- Prefer ruined objects, wrong maps, ledger gaps, rumors, and refusals over complete official accounts.
- Leave holes. Floor plans and full hostile lists are discovered in the world when possible—not fully briefed in one room.
- Institutions speak clean language (“heresy,” “inquiry closed,” “the record”). Dirt lives in subtext.
- Name carefully. Some erase names (“the witch”); don’t dump lore labels a speaker wouldn’t use.

### Scene structure (Sapkowski micro-play)
1. Sensory + intimate hook
2. Concrete want
3. Complication / moral bind before the first third ends
4. Dialogue beats that reveal motive or truth by evasion
5. Close on consequence or ironic image—not full resolution
Motif: rethread a recurring image (bell, flame, seal, cupboard debts, fog)

### Character / relationship check
- Does each speaker sound only like themselves?
- Is tension personal as much as external?
- Does one person know more than they say?

### Output format
1. Verdict (2–4 sentences): Sapkowski + Elden Ring + Witcher-prep feel—or AI/over-explained?
2. Failures (bullets): mystery leaks, voice collapse, env not telling story, dumps, on-nose lines, AND any prep scene that skips knowing-the-monster—with quoted examples.
3. Keep list: what already works.
4. Then either:
   - DIAGNOSE ONLY: ranked fix list, or
   - REVISE: rewrite the scoped passage in full, preserving continuity with character files and earlier chapters.
5. After revise: short note of what was withheld for the reader to discover later—and what monster-questions remain unanswered before they climb.
